Two verbs (move, attack) and real steel. Every outcome comes from simulated bodies and blades; nothing is a canned animation. Numbers live in the rules content file; this doc names mechanisms, not values.

> **Physics computes the fight once.** The deterministic combat plane owns all physical truth. 3D sword placement, body posture, VFX, audio, haptics, combat readability, accessibility cues and tactical labels never reinterpret that fight; they expose the same solved state — impulses, energy, slip, contact identity, blade velocity — through additional spatial and sensory projections (EMERGENT-COMBAT-001, CONTACT-PRESENTATION-001, COMBAT-READABILITY-001).

## Swing semantics: describing a swing without deciding it (COMBAT-009)

Everything above decides outcomes. `SwingSemantics` is the other direction: named readings over authoritative state, for the player watching a blade, the HUD, a training overlay, and the debug vectors. Nothing in the simulation reads one back, and no presentation code recomputes a combat formula — a value not on `PresentationSnapshot` or an event payload is done without rather than invented.

**Swing potential** is how dangerous this part of this swing is, in `[0, 1]`, before anything about the opponent. It is quadratic in the blade's actual tip speed, because cutting is an energy problem and severity goes as `v²`. Charge and arc are deliberately absent from the formula: a charged swing is dangerous because the motor drove the blade faster and further, and that speed is already in the tip speed. Counting it again would make the same physical blade speed mean two different things depending on how it was reached.

Because it reads the blade rather than the input, **the dangerous part of an arc emerges from the acceleration curve instead of being authored**, and lands at a slightly different point every swing. There is a living curve to learn, not "frame 14 is the crit frame". A blade that is not carrying a strike has no potential at all, however fast it happens to be travelling — a wind-back is a fast-moving sword that cannot cut anyone.

**Swing progress** is physical travel along the commanded arc, never elapsed ticks. A sword stopped dead by another sword has stopped progressing; an animation clock would keep counting and tell the player their swing was further along than their blade.

**Potential is not prediction.** Convergence only exists at contact, so nothing before contact may promise what a strike would do. An interface may show the attacker's own potential and let vulnerability read from the target's own physical state — a displaced weapon, a committed swing, a bad angle, carried momentum — which is why each fighter's snapshot carries their own exposure rather than a meter predicting someone else's hit.

**Grades** (`GRAZE`, `LIGHT`, `SOLID`, `HEAVY`, `SWEET`, `DEVASTATING`) name a resolved strike. They describe what the physics produced and never choose it, and there is no dice roll anywhere near them. `SWEET` is the one grade that is not a magnitude: it means convergence, which is exactly what `critical` already decides, so it has no second independent definition that could eventually disagree.

## Cardinal sides and one locked world (SIDE-001)

The arena has a permanently fixed orientation — **north is `+y`, south is `-y`** — and the simulation never rotates for anyone's benefit.

`DuelSide { LIGHT_SOUTH, DARK_NORTH }` is a first-class identity, not a consequence. It decides canonical spawn, initial facing, presentation identity, and the local camera orientation; nothing infers it from slot number, spawn position, colour, or camera yaw. Light spawns at `(0, -spawn_offset)` looking north and Dark at `(0, +spawn_offset)` looking south, each already looking at the other. Quick Play draws sides 50/50 from the match seed, so both cardinal spawns and both camera orientations get exercised by ordinary play instead of the Dark path rotting until someone turns on multiplayer.

The local camera yaw is the one quantity two people watching the same match may legitimately disagree about: the local player is always at the bottom of the screen, so a local Dark player's view is turned half a revolution. That is exactly why it lives in presentation and never in `StateHasher` or replay state — it is what will let two networked players see opposite perspectives of one authoritative world.

Footwork is encoded in each fighter's own combat frame rather than world directions (MOVEMENT-FRAME-001), which is what makes _local right projects right on screen_ and _local forward projects up_ true for both sides once the rig has turned. Without it, one of the two ends would be playing mirrored controls.

Side is communicated by more than colour: the two home ends are different **shapes** as well as different tints, the spawn orientation says it, and the HUD plate names it in words. The arena's cues stay close to the floor tone deliberately — cardinality is orientation, not decoration, and the floor must never compete with blade readability.

## The canonical physical baseline (CONTENT-002)

Everything below is in SI units, measured against one concrete fighter holding one concrete sword. `scale = 1.0` _is_ this pairing; there are no abstract units anywhere.

| Quantity              | Baseline | Scaling                           |
| --------------------- | -------- | --------------------------------- |
| Fighter height        | 1.75 m   | linear in fighter scale           |
| Fighter mass          | 80 kg    | authored; cubic only as a default |
| Fighter body radius   | 0.27 m   | linear in fighter scale           |
| Sword overall length  | 1.22 m   | linear in weapon scale            |
| Sword effective blade | 0.97 m   | linear in weapon scale            |
| Sword mass            | 1.60 kg  | authored; cubic only as a default |
| Canonical guard       | ±45°     | invariant                         |
| Guard limit           | ±135°    | invariant                         |
| Tap arc               | 90°      | invariant                         |

These are a _normalized combat baseline_, not a demographic claim. 1.75 m is close to the modern adult male mean, but 80 kg is deliberately under the ~90 kg population mean, which includes bodies nobody would cast as a duelist. The sword needs no such apology: 1.22 m at 1.60 kg is almost directly represented by surviving museum examples, which makes it the more trustworthy of the two anchors.

The single law that governs all of it:

> **Scale changes physical inputs; physical equations produce gameplay outputs.**

Size, mass, force, and torque feed inertia; inertia feeds acceleration and speed; those feed relative motion; relative motion feeds collision; collision feeds impulse, severity, and exposure; and only then is there a combat result. There is never a step that reads "bigger fighter → +20% damage".

### Fighter mass

Mass resists everything and causes nothing. It sets translational acceleration (`a = F/m`), displacement under impulse, collision response, body contribution to a strike, stability, and rotational inertia. It is **not** a damage multiplier: a heavy fighter does not hit harder with the same sword at the same speed — they are simply harder to move and slower to get going.

### Weapon mass and geometry

Both are authored facts, kept independent (PHYS-005). Geometry scales linearly; mass is measured, not generated, because a longer sword is usually also a thinner one and a cubic rule would quietly invent a crowbar. Cubic mass exists only as an explicit default generator for hypothetical same-density geometry.

### Moment of inertia (PHYS-006)

`I = k · m · L²` for the weapon, `I_body = k_b · m · r²` for the fighter. `k` is the authored mass-distribution coefficient — a uniform rod about its own end is `1/3`, a tip-heavy weapon more, a pommel-weighted one less. A hand check on the baseline sword gives `⅓ × 1.6 × 1.22² ≈ 0.794 kg·m²`; the shipped blade sits a little above that because its mass starts at the hilt rather than at the pommel.

This is the sword's own moment. The weapon coordinate θ has one inertia built on it — that moment, plus the sword's mass riding the hilt track and the hands on the grip, at the current pose — and the motor divides its torque by exactly the inertia every blade contact divides its impulse by (UNIFIED-MASS-001). Nothing authors an angular acceleration beside it: the two would drift apart and the blade would stop obeying its own mass.

### Force and torque: authored actuators

Strength is authored as **force and torque**, never acceleration. A fighter's resolved actuators are `locomotion_force`, `braking_force`, `burst_force`, `turn_torque` and `weapon_torque_scale`; the accelerations are quotients against that fighter's own mass and inertia. Content authors them as three scales relative to the reference duelist (1.0 each) — `strength_scale` (weapon drive), `traction_scale` (locomotion, braking and burst force), `turn_support_scale` (heading turn torque) — and `FighterCatalog.resolve_actuators` turns them into newtons and newton-metres when the definition is built. The simulation never reads a scale. Strength is never inferred from size: a big fighter is strong only if it is authored strong. The old "larger is stronger but slower" laws (mass `s³`, force `s²`, so `a = F/m` goes as `1/s`) survive only as the explicit prototype generator `FighterCatalog.prototype_at(size)`. No speed penalty is authored anywhere, and none may be.

`weapon_torque_scale` is how the same sword behaves differently in different hands — it scales the wielder's applied torque. It never alters the weapon's mass or inertia, which are properties of the object.

## One engine, any combatant (COMBATANT-GENERIC-001, LOADOUT-001)

The physics engine does not know who is fighting. Each fighter is a `FighterState` driven through the same systems by its own `CombatantPhysicsSpec`, resolved once before tick 0:

| Part                            | Owns                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `FighterDefinition` (character) | body — mass, height, push / hurt / hand geometry, inertia, lying body — and resolved actuators   |
| `WeaponDefinition`              | the object — geometry, mass distribution, grip stations, contact material, motor torques, damage |
| `WeaponHandlingProfile`         | this body holding this weapon — the hilt track `C(θ)` and its carriage                           |

`DuelRules.combatants` holds one spec per slot; systems ask `rules.spec_of(fighter)`. Every pair quantity uses both sides' own values — push radii summed, each real mass, the attacker's blade against the target's torso, each sword's own features and material — so a light fighter accelerates more under the same force, a light sword turns faster under the same torque, and a long sword threatens a target a short one cannot reach, with no branch anywhere. A fighter/weapon pair with no authored handling fails closed; nothing ever falls back to the reference pair. The reference duelist remains the calibration content the injury constants, the clash yardstick and the CPU's calibration reach were measured on — never infrastructure. The replay header pins each slot's loadout ids and a fingerprint of its resolved content.

Scope: any fighter carrying one rigid compound weapon on the one-coordinate θ model (a sword, rapier, axe, spear). Flexible, articulated or dual-wielded weapons would need a different motion model and are not pre-built.

Calibration order matters, because it is the only order in which the numbers mean anything: dimensions, then locomotion force, then turn torque against body inertia, then sword torque, then structural coupling, and the damage curve **last**. A baseline that feels right is never achieved by picking a damage number first.

## Sword physics reference

One page of what the sword _is_ in the simulation, from the angle a player commands to the damage a contact deals. Values live in `game/content/rules/`; this names the mechanisms and where they are implemented.

**One coordinate.** The weapon angle `θ ∈ [−guard_limit, +guard_limit]` is the only weapon state (WEAPON-ORBIT-001). The blade's world yaw is exactly `φ + θ`, where `φ` is the fighter's CombatHeading (`FighterState.facing`) and not the target bearing `τ` (HEADING-001). Its world direction is that heading's rotation of the combat-frame blade, so the two headings of a mirrored duel point exactly opposite ways (WEAPON-ORBIT-004). The 270° range is the weapon's angular control and guard domain; it is not a hitbox (SLASH-SHAPE-001).

**The hilt track.** For every `θ`, the two-handed carriage (grip centre) rides the hilt track `C(θ)` in the combat frame (WEAPON-PATH-001/003) — the fighter↔weapon handling's, not the fighter's or the weapon's (LOADOUT-001): a few authored samples on one side (Long Guard on the centreline, the point corridor, Ready, Gather, Load), joined by a monotone cubic Hermite, mirrored exactly (forward even in θ, lateral odd), baked once into a lookup with its slope `C′(θ)` and read between samples as the cubic Hermite through them, so the slope a contact is solved with is the derivative of the carriage the sweep moves (rules 51, BLADE-COLLISION-PARITY-001). The track never turns the blade; `θ` does. Long Guard is the farthest-forward posture (LONG-GUARD-GEOMETRY-001). Rules load fails closed on a track whose hands or sword would sit inside the wielder's torso.

**Two representations of one sword** (STEEL-TOPOLOGY-001, GUARD-PROTECTION-001, BLADE-COLLISION-PARITY-001, TORSO-CIRCLE-001). Moving the sword is the full model: `WeaponKinematics` builds the whole sword — pommel, handle, crossguard, blade — from `θ` and `C(θ)`, and that one pose drives damage, leverage, and the drawn sword. The hands and the handle position the sword; they are not a target. Rigid blocking is simpler: each sword's steel is its blade capsule (from the guard root to the point) and its guard root, a small convex wedge filling the corner between crossbar and blade that is steel only against a blade coming from the blade side. Three steel pairs — blade on blade, and each blade on the other's guard root, at most two points each — built from that same pose and moving with the same point velocities; two crossguards never meet. Damage comes only from the blade, swept continuously through the tick (substepped CCD in one chronological TOI loop, COMBAT-002/007). The slash's visible shape is the union of the moving rigid blade; there is no attack circle, authored hitbox or animation-driven collider. What the blade can wound is the torso circle. The crossguard blocks from its blade side and is never wounded; from the grip side an enemy blade passes it and wounds only if that same blade track then enters the torso circle.

**Sword parts (glossary).** _Blade_: the edge and point, base to tip — steel from the guard root's apex to the point. _Crossguard_: the bar at the blade's base; with the corner it makes with the blade, the _guard root_ — steel on its blade side only (rules 50–51: visual only). _Guard line_: the bar's line across the sword; which side of it an attacking blade's line crosses the sword decides whether the guard root is steel to it. _Handle_: pommel to crossguard; drawn, not a hurt shape. _Hands_: two discs on the handle's grip markers; drawn, and they clear the wielder's own torso, not a hurt shape. _Grip_: the hands and the handle together, a positioning name, not a wound region (TORSO-CIRCLE-001). _Pommel_: the handle's end, where blade radii are measured from. _Guard_ names a posture or a stance, never a sword part, and the names are not one angle. The Long Guard posture is the centred blade at θ = 0, the longest reach. The neutral return target is the angle the motor tracks when nothing stronger governs it (`stable_side × guard_angle`). The settled neutral guard is the equilibrium that motor actually holds; with the return active and no other drive, that is the return target, not the centred posture. Nothing in the current control law holds θ = 0 against that return. _Hilt_ is retired as a part name; the _hilt track_ is still the name of the carriage's path `C(θ)`.

**Contact velocity, lever and effective mass.** A blade point moves with the body, the heading's rotation, the blade's rotation and the carriage sliding along its track (`v = v_body + φ̇·perp(r) + θ̇·(R(φ)·C′(θ) + perp(û·d))`, PHYS-007). The lever `c` is that motion per unit `θ̇` along the contact normal (`WeaponKinematics.lever`). Each sword side resists a contact with `1/m_body + c²/M_θ`, where `M_θ` is the held sword's generalized inertia at this pose (the sword plus the hands at the grip, `WeaponKinematics.held_inertia`); a struck body resists with `1/m`. One solved impulse `J` pushes the target, recoils the attacker and turns the sword by `J·c/M_θ` (IMPACT-PHYSICS-001). Friction is Coulomb on every sword row (BLADE-FRICTION-001).

**Leverage and the sweet spot are emergent.** Near the hands the lever vanishes and the bodies carry the contact; toward the tip the lever is long, the effective mass small, and the sword deflects easily. The sweet spot is the sword's centre of percussion about the hands — a derived weapon property — and the peak of a loss-only cut-efficiency profile. Both depend on `θ` (through `C′(θ)` and `M_θ`) and on the live body and heading motion, so they move while the fight moves. Strong, middle and weak are readings; nothing multiplies a force or damage by a blade zone (EMERGENT-COMBAT-001).

**Energy and damage.** Severity is the kinetic energy the contact solve dissipated. Damage reads it against the injury calibration — named, unit-bearing, frozen constants (`REFERENCE_CUT_ENERGY_J`, `REFERENCE_THRUST_ENERGY_J`, `REFERENCE_STAGGER_IMPULSE_NS`, seeded from the rules-39 reference strike; at rules 49 that strike — a square cut at the centre of percussion at 10.5 m/s — was re-seeded as two reference-quality wounds, so a clean fast cut is decisive and a slow one stays a scratch) — scaled only by loss-only factors (cut efficiency, edge or incidence validity) and a compressive curve. A slower attack delivers less; the same energy always does the same to a body. Charge reaches damage only through the speed the motor built.

**The actuator.** Every weapon phase is a bounded torque driving `θ̇` toward a target speed (swing, brake, windup, hold), never an assigned speed, divided by the same `M_θ` the contacts use (UNIFIED-MASS-001), so the hilt track and the hands weigh on the motor exactly as they weigh on a clash. Charge is earned wind-back (COMBAT-004) and sets the arc and the target speed (tap 10.5, half 14.25, full 18 rad/s for the reference sword, linear in charge); a probe is stopped harder than a committed cut, which is why a full cut carries farther after a miss.

**Commitment, sidestep, whiff, recovery.** Commitment spends authority, never existing momentum (PHYS-003). Footwork's authority reads the weapon's commitment `K`; the heading's reads **directional commitment** — physical _redirect debt_ (DIRECTIONAL-COMMIT-001, COMMITMENT-001, rules 46): the weapon's momentum `M_θ·θ̇` plus the drive its motor is still committing over 0.1 s, against its brake torque; the body's momentum along its heading plus the force its footwork is still applying, against its braking force, signed (forward +, retreat −) — each mapped smoothly as `t / (t + T)` against the fighter's healthy braking, so there is no edge to cross and the wound is never counted into the debt; one bounded composition for the heading, which at full commitment keeps 35 % of its turn torque and 50 % of its turn rate, and with injury on top never less than 25 % / 40 % of healthy (RESPONSIVE-COMMITMENT-001). Input is never delayed by it: a committed fighter asked to turn begins on that tick. Advancing adds exactly the body's velocity to the strike's closing speed through the one point velocity — never a multiplier. Power and forward momentum buy threat and cost redirection; lateral footwork costs nothing; backing off gives some back but never more than neutral. There is no authored overswing, whiff or counter-rotation penalty, and no reset when a parry arrests the blade — the commitment falls with the blade's speed. A side-step works because the weapon frame hangs on `φ`, which a committed fighter cannot swing onto the target — and from rules 47 the feet ride the same frame (MOVEMENT-FRAME-001), so a committed fighter keeps driving down its own line while `φ` comes round instead of steering its body onto the target; `τ` reaches the feet only through the heading motor. Two measured limits shape what that buys (rules 46): a tap reaches the body in about 0.1 s across ±45° of `φ`, more bearing than any reactive step covers, so a tap in measure is beaten by spacing, not by a lateral reaction; and a full cut sweeps 270°, so it is beaten by distance before the release. Measured at rules 47 through real input: a back-dash taken as an attacker reaches the measure it last saw makes its tap miss clean; a guard wound into the tap's path parries it and the released wind ripostes; and giving ground on a back-diagonal dash with the sword wound out of the sweep makes a full advancing cut whiff from 1.2–1.5× reach — but that bait needs 2.4–3.0 m of space, and the attacker's own recovery returns its guard before the gap can be closed, so the whiff is not followed by a counter. That last gap is a design finding, not a defect to tune away. A whiff is a label, never a mechanic (WHIFF-001): physics produced a miss — the blade reached its endpoint without having met the opponent with a solved impulse — and only then is `ATTACK_WHIFFED` emitted, once, for presentation and telemetry. Nothing in the simulation or the CPU reads it. A landed hit that carries on into its overswing is a hit. Recovery is a six-tick floor plus the blade back under `control_speed` on its own brake (RECOVERY-001), so the opening a whiff leaves is whatever `θ`, `θ̇`, heading error and remaining authority leave — no authored punish window. Input is never refused for it (AUTHORITY-001): a committed fighter can always ask to turn, back up or redirect, and physics decides how fast they can comply.

**Threat and exposure.** `InitiativeModel.estimate` is one bounded 30-tick prediction of the attacker's actual blade capsule against the target's hurt circle, under the attacker's own bounded motor, heading and footwork, each condition-scaled (THREAT-001). A weapon not yet ready moves with the footwork, so a resting guard walked into a torso threatens. It does not predict the opponent's sword: a guard makes an attack defendable, not unreachable. Inside the horizon it gives a real time; past it, it gives the horizon tagged as such and keeps only what was left (the blade's gap to the torso, the readiness still owed) for ranking — never invented seconds. `ExposureModel` reads the margin between the two fighters' threat times; the riposte advantage a parry carries, the CPU, the HUD and the debug overlay are all readings of the one predictor, never damage (PHYS-004).

**Parry.** Physical interception (PARRY-001): the defender's sword met the cut before any body, with a real impulse, and arrested it — brought it back under the attacker's `control_speed` (the one arrest law, RIGID-CONTACT-002). The riposte advantage is a separate reading; a parry may win no tempo at all.

**One solve, many words (CONTACT-OUTCOME-001).** Every sword contact is one solve — the same geometry, manifold, masses, `Jn`/`Jt`, friction and actuator limits — and `ContactOutcome` describes what it did: the impulse each sword received (`Σ Jn·n + Jt·t`), friction saturation, the rows' closing before and after, each fighter's `ΔV` and `Δθ̇`, which cut was arrested, the attack's progress before and after, and whether the cut was on the line. The solver never reads it. The words are readings of it and of what the attack then did:

| Reading       | What physically happened                                                                                        |
| ------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| PARRY         | protective steel, positive impulse, the cut arrested                                                            |
| DEFLECTION    | protective steel, positive impulse, a cut on the line not arrested — and the attack ended having struck nothing |
| BIND / block  | protective contact persisting under pressure (a block is a parry that stays loaded; it is not an event)         |
| carry-through | contact too weak to change the outcome: the strike lands                                                        |
| WHIFF         | no contact at all                                                                                               |
| RIPOSTE       | an ordinary attack from the state a defense left — no mode, window or bonus (RIPOSTE-001)                       |

`ContactResolver.classify` is a different word. The event's `class` is the closing-speed band used for the clang: `light` below `blade_solid_speed` (3 m/s), `solid` from there up to `blade_strong_speed` (8 m/s), `strong` at and above that. It does not say whether the cut was stopped. `ContactReading` does: a cut that keeps at least half its progress is a glance, including when `class` is `solid`. The app journey `test_a_tap_glancing_off_a_guard_is_redirected_but_not_stopped` asserts both — `solid`, and `ContactReading.GLANCE`, and not arrested. One such contact is not a parry, a bind, or a fast crossing.

The event's `energy` is the drop in the two swords' kinetic energy across that solve, floored at 0. It is not the impulse. A redirect can carry a large impulse and still dissipate about nothing, because the momentum moved and the energy came back. That reading is not, by itself, a defect, and it is not a reason to change friction or the impulse law.

Because the reduced model sweeps θ about φ, a contact cannot bend a cut's path; it slows it, shoves the bodies, or stops it. A deflection is therefore a contact whose delay or shove let the remaining sweep miss — usually a defender stepping off the line while their steel takes the cut — and it is decided when the attack ends, from what actually happened, never predicted at the contact (DEFLECTION-001). Its direction is the blades' own impulse, never a direction built from facing. None of these words is read back by the simulation or the CPU, and PARRY does not mean the defender won: the advantage reading (attacker's time-to-threat minus the defender's) says whether the contact bought any tempo.

**A won line is physical (DEFLECTION-CONSEQUENCE-001).** No contact names a winner. The solver's impulses (normal and friction, at the point of contact, through each sword's lever and generalized inertia) leave each fighter with a resulting θ, θ̇, φ, φ̇ and V, and that state is the whole consequence. A fighter whose sword was knocked off the line, or is still moving away from it, has _redirect debt_: the bounded motor must arrest that momentum, reverse it and drive a new cut, and that takes real time. So a hard beat that changes `M_θ·θ̇` a lot leaves a long opening, a glance leaves almost none, and a whiff obeys the same law because nothing stopped its momentum. Where the defender caught the cut sets the size of the effect without any multiplier: near its own forte the defender's sword barely moves and the attacker's changes a lot; near its weak both move; an oblique contact mostly slides. There is no parry recovery, no whiff recovery, no knock-back and no strong/weak parry factor, and restitution is not the opening lever. PARRY, WHIFF and the riposte advantage describe the result and never select it (NO-LABEL-PHYSICS-001); presentation may scale clang, sparks, recoil and camera impulse from the solved impulse.

**Four readings, never interchangeable (THREAT-READINESS-001).**

| Reading     | Question                                                 | Source                                                                   |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Commitment  | How hard is present motion to redirect?                  | Redirect debt (DIRECTIONAL-COMMIT-001)                                   |
| Readiness   | How soon can this state threaten this opponent?          | `InitiativeModel` time-to-threat from θ, θ̇, φ, φ̇, X, V and the actuators |
| Preparation | How much launch authority does this blade position give? | `\|θ\| / guard_angle` at release (COMBAT-005)                            |
| Advantage   | Who threatens first after an exchange, and by how much?  | One readiness minus the other; a measurement                             |

A sword can be heavily committed and already threatening, or sitting at an ideal load angle while flying the wrong way and therefore not ready. Treating `|θ|` as readiness is the error this table exists to prevent.

**Input is immediate; redirection has inertia.** However large the debt, TURN, MOVE and ATTACK are accepted on their tick (AUTHORITY-001); the fighter simply takes time to comply. Old feel is never recovered by making control sluggish.

**Injury.** Damage degrades the fighter's voluntary actuators through `ConditionState` (CONDITION-001): force `0.70 + 0.30h`, speed `0.85 + 0.15h`, tracking `0.80 + 0.20h`, frozen once per tick from health. It never changes mass, inertia, geometry, momentum or perception, and every command still lands on its tick.

## Attack language (COMBAT-001)

- **Tap**: release before the threshold → exactly 0% charge, and a 90° cut **from wherever the blade actually is**. +45° commands −45°, but +20° commands −70° and +135° commands +45°. A tap is a rotation, never a move to the opposite guard.
- **Hold**: winds the blade physically away from the swing under bounded torque; **release** swings from the actual retracted angle with arc `min_arc + earned wind-back`.
- **Direction**: from the side the blade is _committed_ to (`stable_side`), not the live sign of the angle, so a blade hovering near centre cannot alternate on noise. The sword rests where it stopped, so the next cut starts there.
- **Phases** (`CombatPhase`): `NEUTRAL → CHARGING → LAUNCH → ACTIVE_EARLY/THREAT/LATE → OVERSWING → RECOVERY`, plus `STAGGER`, `DEAD`. There is no `BIND` phase: a bind is a reading of a contact that persists, never a weapon state (RIGID-CONTACT-002).
- **Cancel**: a canceled input drops a charge into a short recovery, never an attack.

**Recovery length is physical, and that is the skill (RECOVERY-001).** The `RECOVERY` phase is not a cooldown and has no duration table: it ends when a universal six-tick floor has passed **and** the blade is back under `control_speed`, arrested by the attack's own brake torque. A tap — little momentum, a strong brake — clears the gate almost at once; a full haymaker overswinging on its weak brake takes longer; a body spun off line or off balance pays through the turn torque and movement authority it has left, not through extra recovery ticks. Nothing counts the same physics twice. This is Riposte's depth analogue to a fighting-game tech like L-cancelling — but it has **no button and no cancel input**: the player earns the short recovery by throwing a strike their brake can stop, so the reward is in the physics. Presentation reads `recovery_remaining01` (the recovery's own commitment decay) to carry the finish pose; it never shortens the recovery.

**Hitstop never freezes the player's intention.** During a hitstop hold the fixed-tick driver stops feeding ticks (wall-clock only, HITSTOP-001), but the application keeps polling held movement every display frame, so a direction or charge the player changes mid-freeze is already in effect when ticks resume — the motor acts on the current intention, not a stale one. Hitstop does not buffer an _attack_ into execution; it only lets the standing movement intention stay live, so the freeze reads as weight rather than as a dropped input.

## Charge is earned displacement (COMBAT-004)

Charge is not a timer. It is the outward travel a hold actually generated, measured past a baseline of `max(guard_angle, |hold_start_angle|)`:

```text
earned_windback = max(0, |angle| - max(guard_angle, |hold_start_angle|))
charge          = earned_windback / windback_span()
arc             = min_arc + earned_windback
```

Four consequences follow without a single special case:

| Situation                                            | Earns   | Why                                                  |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Hold from +20° out to +45°                           | nothing | restoring an under-prepared blade is not preparation |
| A clash leaves the blade at +100°, press and release | nothing | the baseline is where the hold started               |
| Hold from +100° out to +135°                         | 35°     | only the travel the hold added                       |
| Hold at +135° forever                                | nothing | a blade with nowhere to go cannot charge             |

Preparation (`|angle| / guard_angle`, saturating at the canonical guard) is separate: it caps the angular velocity a swing can reach, is captured once at release as `launch_preparation`, and is never a damage multiplier. Inside ±`guard_angle` the blade is quick but under-loaded; outside it has full authority but a longer arc before it threatens. Neither is uniformly better. An idle blade, and a recovery that is back under control, is carried toward its committed guard by a capped pose tracker (GUARD-RETURN-001); the angle is never snapped. Each new steel episode dips that return by the sword's own change in angular velocity, down to a floor, and the deficit then fades at the return's natural frequency. A torso entry does not dip it. A later steel episode can dip it again (IMPACT-AUTHORITY-001). Preparation is **not readiness**: where a blade sits says nothing about which way it is moving (THREAT-READINESS-001, below).

**The domain is not the attack (ATTACK-ARC-001).** The sword can turn anywhere in ±135° (270°), but one attack travels its `arc`: 90° for a tap, 180° at most for a full charge (`max_arc = π`). A full right attack runs −135° → +45°, a full left one +135° → −45°; together they cover the domain, and neither sweeps it.

## Guard classification (COMBAT-005)

`GuardRegion` labels a blade `UNDER_PREPARED` / `BASELINE` / `OUTWARD` for the HUD, telemetry, and debug. It is a readout, not a rule: lint forbids `src/domain/combat` and `src/domain/match` from referencing it, because physics must branch on continuous angle, preparation, and earned wind-back. Physics drives classification, never the reverse.

## Relational physics (PHYS-001 – PHYS-004)

The central law: **the same sword swing produces different consequences because both fighters participate in the collision.** Movement changes collision physics; it never applies a damage modifier.

```text
CHARGE → MOTOR WORK → θ̇                     (charge never multiplies a hit)
BODY MOTION + HEADING TURN + SWORD ROTATION + CARRIAGE ALONG ITS ORBIT
        ↓  v_point = v_fighter + φ̇·perp(r) + θ̇·(C′(θ) + perp(R(θ)·r))
EXACT CONTACT-POINT VELOCITY  (and its blade fraction: 0 guard, 1 tip)
        ↓  minus the other side's velocity there, projected on the normal
RELATIVE CONTACT VELOCITY v_n
        ↓  contact inverse effective mass k = Σ sides (1/m_body + c²/M_θ) [+ 1/m_resist]
IMPULSE  J = (1 + e)·v_n / k         and   ENERGY  E = ½(1 − e²)·v_n² / k
        ↓  edge alignment, cut efficiency (a loss, peaked at the centre of percussion)
PHYSICAL SEVERITY
        ↓  (exposure is reported beside it as a reading of initiative — never a factor)
DAMAGE / STAGGER / RECOVERY CONSEQUENCE
```

**One contact mechanics** (IMPACT-PHYSICS-001). Every sword contact — blade on blade, blade into a body, the persistent sword-body constraint — is solved by `ContactMechanics`: each sword-holding side is its real body mass plus its held weapon inertia through the Jacobian `c` at the exact contact point (`WeaponKinematics.lever`), a struck body is that same real mass, and the impulse maps back into both through the same terms. Held inertia is the sword plus the hands on the grip. Plant and commitment do not change it. There is no authored body share and no Strong/Weak, parry or tip multiplier. Where the blade is struck changes `c`, the point's speed, and therefore the effective mass. That gradient is the strong and the weak when the geometry produces it; it is not a separate curve the solver is required to invent. A shorter lever displaces its sword less than a longer one. The centre of percussion is derived from the mass distribution. Cut efficiency is a loss in `(0, 1]` and cannot add energy.

**One contact, many readings.** A point is not a separate physics: a POKE or THRUST is solved with the same `k`, weapon term included, as a cut. Driven straight along its blade a point's lever is zero (the rotation moves the point across the axis, and at Long Guard the carriage moves nothing along it), so the sword barely turns and the point drives in; the same point met off its axis has a real lever, the blade gives way, and less energy goes into the body. Classification only decides how the result is read — which reference normalizes it and which loss applies — never which mass model computes it.

**Impulse and severity are different quantities.** Impulse drives displacement, blade deflection, stagger, and camera kick. Severity drives cutting, injury, and lethality: every contact reads the energy it dissipates, `E_diss`, against its own reference (a cut's or a thrust's). `E_diss` is the physical ceiling, not the injury: some of it really goes into flex, ringing and moving the target, which the rigid model leaves out, so damage scales it by loss-only factors in `(0, 1]` — the blade's bite at the contact and how valid the contact was (the edge leading a cut, the point entering square for a thrust). The edge's out-of-plane alignment is not simulated: in an active slash it is assumed to lead, and the presentation's X cut honours that plane. Letting one scalar do both is what makes weapons feel like stat blocks.

From the same `k`, `J·v_n = 2E / (1 − e)`, so the two disagree in a way that is worth stating: for the same cutting energy, the **slower** strike carries more momentum. So a moderate swing with the body behind it shoves a fighter out of position, and a vicious fast one opens them up without moving them much. Each consequence reads the quantity that actually causes it:

| Consequence             | Driven by                                                         |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Body displacement       | `Δv = J / m_resisting` — no knockback constant, no exposure       |
| Stagger                 | normalized impulse                                                |
| Attacker's swing arrest | normalized impulse (the momentum it gave away)                    |
| Damage and lethality    | `E_diss` ÷ its reference × bite × edge (cut) or incidence (point) |
| Critical classification | physical severity plus blade region and alignment (convergence)   |

**The sweet spot is a weapon property, not a bonus.** The sword's centre of percussion about the hands, `I_grip / (m·a)`, is derived from its mass distribution and where it is held (0.633 of the bastard sword's blade about the middle of Wolf's two hands); struck there it turns about the hands without jarring them. The cut-efficiency profile peaks there and stays in `(0, 1]`: it models what a rigid sword leaves out — vibration and a poor bite near the hilt and the very point — and can never add energy. The impulse and the energy are the contact's whatever the profile says. The references that normalize impulse and severity (`reference_impulse`, `reference_severity`, `reference_thrust_severity`) are this same mechanics applied to a reference strike, so "1.0" always means that strike. The centre of percussion is a reference point where impact and hand reaction agree, not "where the sword hits hardest": near the guard the contact is heavy (most impulse, least energy), near the point light and fast (most speed, least control), and the sweet spot emerges from velocity × effective mass × leverage × bite, never from a bonus. The slash ribbon draws the wider band (`SwingSemantics.SWEET_REGION_MIN/MAX`, `0.55`–`0.90`) and a separate glint at `percussion_fraction`. It does not draw `WeaponKinematics.lever`. Current drawing: [presentation](./presentation.md#slash-ribbon-sweet-region-and-leverage).

**There is no universal sword force.** The same pre-impact energy gives wildly different peak forces depending on how long the contact lasts and what gives way — a 23 J transverse blow stopped over 5 cm is roughly 900 N; stop it over half that and the force doubles. So nothing in the rules authors, calibrates against or reads a force. Velocity, effective mass, impulse and energy are the primary quantities; average force `J/Δt` exists only for instrumentation and presentation — it changes with the substep, the momentum does not.

**Calibration against the real thing.** The reference pair (CONTENT-002) is the baseline duelist — presented as Wolf, 1.75 m and 80 kg — holding the bastard sword: 1.22 m overall, 0.97 m of blade, 1.60 kg. Museum hand-and-a-half swords cluster around 1.1–1.3 m and 1.3–2.0 kg (the Royal Armouries' featured example is about 1.20 m and 1.8 kg), so the pair is squarely historical. The fastest sword-point speed measured across longsword practitioners in a 2019 inertial-sensor study was about 14 m/s, and roughly a kilogram of effective mass at that speed is about 100 J — the band the reference strike sits in (49 J at the guard, 94 J at the centre of percussion, 97 J at the tip at θ̇ = 15 rad/s). These are calibration witnesses, not targets: a number is never changed because it "sounds large", only because the physics or the play says so.

Damage curves are **compressive** at the top. Severity is genuinely quadratic in closing speed, but injury is not: a cut that already opens a fighter up is not improved by more energy. Without that compression the quadratic would make heavy charges strictly dominant and spacing, timing, and punishment would stop paying.

**Effective mass is not body mass** (PHYS-002). A bounded `structural_coupling ∈ [0, 1]` decides how much body inertia is actually behind the blade, derived from plant quality, how coherently body motion supports the strike, body angular coherence, and acceleration debt. Commitment and coupling are different questions — "how hard is this to change?" versus "how well is the body supporting it?" — and a fully committed swing made mid-sidestep is extremely committed _and_ badly structured.

`StructuralCoupling.effective_mass(share, rules)` is the one place that formula is written, and it takes the coupling rather than the fighter: the caller that resolves a hit also _reports_ the coupling in the strike event, and deriving it twice is how the number shown and the number used come to disagree.

| Style                   | Gains                                                                            | Costs                                                                   |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Planted                 | highest coupling, control, rotational authority, deflection resistance, recovery | no closing contribution; target can retreat out of measure              |
| Advancing               | translational blade velocity, closing speed, often good coupling                 | forward momentum, weak braking and lateral correction, large whiff wake |
| Retreating              | measure, lower incoming closing speed, counter timing                            | less own contribution, weaker coupling while actively backpedalling     |
| Abrupt mid-swing change | positional escape                                                                | lowest coupling                                                         |

Retreating therefore carries **no blanket damage penalty**: a retreating fighter whose opponent charges into the blade can land the hardest strike in the duel, because the closing velocity is relational. The same symmetry makes charging recklessly into a developed swing dangerous rather than automatically jamming it — jamming works only on _early_ entry, inside the useful radius before dangerous blade velocity develops.

**Commitment spends the ability to change the future, not the present** (PHYS-003). Rising commitment reduces desired acceleration and corrective turn torque; it never scales existing velocity or angular velocity. Facing resolves as `α = τ/I` against current angular momentum, so a body already rotating into its swing must first arrest that rotation — reversal costs time, and overshoot is emergent rather than scripted. Since rules 36 commitment is one continuous, mirrored reading of the swing: a held charge carries a fifth of the charge's weight; through the cut it builds toward the full weight as the blade actually travels along its arc and gets up to speed (so a blade stopped at its start is still easy to redirect, and no phase boundary steps it); overswing holds it full; recovery lets it go linearly. Nothing reads a side.

**A whiff is purely physical.** There is no whiff penalty term: an unarrested cut runs on, the overswing grows, commitment stays full, and recovery is longer because the real state — overswing depth, commitment, facing, balance — is worse.

**Exposure is a reading, not a multiplier** (PHYS-004, rules 36): how far behind the opponent a fighter is in time-to-threat — their own readiness (weapon state, heading, the sweep onto the line, closing) against the opponent's actual threat. Even initiative reads one half. It feeds the CPU, telemetry, the HUD and presentation, and never damage, stagger or criticals: a fighter who whiffs is punished because the opponent can physically hit first, not because their flesh got softer.

## Contact (COMBAT-002, COMBAT-003, COMBAT-007)

### Sword contact in one plane: points, sliding, friction, binds (PLANAR-BIND-001)

The authoritative duel is one horizontal plane, so swords meet as rigid shapes in that plane and nothing else:

| Geometry                            | Collides                 | Defends (can parry) | Wounds                           |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Blade (capsule, 2.2 cm)             | yes — steel              | yes                 | yes — the only offensive surface |
| Crossguard (bar, 14.4 cm each side) | from its blade side only | yes, from that side | no                               |
| Handle (1.5 cm, its real size)      | no                       | no                  | no                               |
| Hands (3.5 cm discs)                | no                       | no                  | no                               |

The blade is the only offensive surface; the blade, and the guard root from the blade side, are the steel and the defence (STEEL-TOPOLOGY-001, GUARD-PROTECTION-001, DEFENSE-ROLE-001). Which side a blade comes from is read once, as it first touches the guard root: where its line crosses the sword, past the bar's own thickness toward the point (protected) or toward the pommel (passing). Level with the bar is never protection, a blade near parallel to the sword reads its point nearest the bar, and the reading holds until the two are apart, so a blade that came in under the guard keeps passing even after its line rises above the bar. The one release goes the other way: a protected blade that slips off round the quillon's end to the grip side is let go, so the guard never holds a blade against its own underside. A protected contact is ordinary steel: it can be a parry or a deflection only by the same physical tests as blade on blade. Hands and the handle are not combat geometry (TORSO-CIRCLE-001): a blade that crosses them and misses the torso circle does not wound, and it does not stop.

**Contact points, not areas.** Two touching blades are at most two points (`SwordContactSet`): a crossing is one point, an overlap along the blades is its two ends — the standard compact stand-in for a contact patch. Each point carries a normal and a Coulomb tangent impulse and a geometric identity (where along each blade), so a contact sliding along the blade keeps its history and is warm-started from it. There is no surface-area term: Coulomb friction does not depend on apparent area, and the two endpoints already give a contact span. Line pressure (`ΣJn / (dt · span)`) is a fair reading for sparks, grind audio and telemetry; it never feeds back into the solve.

**Friction is material, not orientation.** Blade on blade μ = 0.20; blade on a body μ = 0.05. (Rules 35–49's crossguard and handle material, μ = 0.40, went with that steel at rules 50.) One coefficient per pair, solved on the accumulated tangent impulse: a contact sticks until its demand exceeds `μ·Jn`, then slides — the static/kinetic behaviour falls out of the accumulated Coulomb cone without a second coefficient. Edge-on-edge versus edge-on-flat is not modelled: blade roll and pitch are presentation, and making them change μ would let presentation decide gameplay.

**Binds are equilibrium.** When the normal constraints forbid penetration and neither fighter applies useful tangent motion, weapon torque or footwork, the swords stay bound — for as long as the geometry and the motors keep them so. A guard return can draw an idle blade off a contact whose pose is not the committed guard; that is the motor, not a release rule, and it is not true of every idle overlap. Binding, sliding, tangling and disengaging are readings of the manifold (PLANAR-BIND-001), never states that change it. A bind is broken the way a fencer breaks one: winding the blade, stepping off the line, backing out, or yielding. The canonical witness is a resting overlap on the committed guard: idle keeps it, and backing off, a lateral step, or winding the blade takes it apart (BLADE-ESCAPE-001). An opponent can keep a bind loaded by pressing in or by pursuing a retreat at its pace: measured in real-app play against the Hard CPU, every bind a scripted human could not back out of was one the CPU kept loaded by walking after it — idle, the same human back-off separated all of them — and none held stale contact. Over 120 live wander binds that stay held when both fighters are idle (rules 51), the quickest ordinary escape takes 7 ticks at the median. The slowest (48 ticks) is a fighter still staggered by a hit, its blade lying square across the other: idle, the two points rest on each other with no load; backing off or stepping aside drags its blade along the other's under real normal impulse until the contact runs off the end, and its wind input never takes effect because a staggered weapon cannot start a charge. Slow, physically maintained, and nothing stale. A jam is a defect only when the manifold stops answering changed input, never because it lasted.

**The heading is stance-supported (CONTACT-HEADING-001).** The combat heading φ is not a free body yaw: the feet support it and the bounded turn motor drives it, so a contact's reaction torque about the root is taken by the stance. Contact impulses move the root's translation and the weapon coordinate only; a clean hit never spins the striker's heading, and a planted fighter stays planted. (Treating φ as a free coordinate would need the exact coupled φ–θ inertia — θ is a hinge between body and sword — and is deliberately not the model.) Contact still changes later facing — indirectly: it moves the bodies (so the target bearing τ the heading tracks) and changes the sword's momentum and the motor's committed drive (so the redirect debt the heading turns against). A beat that kills a cut's momentum leaves its wielder able to turn sooner; one that shoves the defender aside leaves the attacker a new bearing to reacquire.

Three intentionally different coupling models govern all contacts:

| Interaction   | A affects B | B affects A       | Meaning                                                               |
| ------------- | ----------- | ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sword ↔ Sword | Yes         | Yes               | True bilateral weapon collision                                       |
| Body ↔ Body   | Yes         | Yes               | Mass-aware physical collision                                         |
| Sword → Body  | Yes         | Through its lever | The blade reacts by `J·c/M_θ`; a centred point has `c ≈ 0` (PHYS-008) |

- Substeps per tick are sized so no blade point travels farther than `substep_travel` while the count stays under `max_substeps`. The sweep samples interpolated poses. It is not an analytic rotational time of impact. Inside the cap, that sample step is narrower than two blade radii, which is the no-tunnel bound on the sampled path. A graze thinner than the sample is not proved by the bound. The witnesses in [testing.md](../reference/testing.md) name the envelope that was actually tried: modest and near-cap tip grazes, both blades turning, a clear twin for each, and a counted overflow past the cap. A full swing and a 2.5× knock stay inside the budget. Past the cap the overflow is counted and the sweep still runs at `max_substeps`, rather than growing a second solver. An exact TOI tie between a defensive blade contact and a weapon→body contact resolves as defense-wins.
- **A tick is resolved chronologically, not all at once.** All contact types — blade↔blade, blade→body, tip→body, and body↔body — share one unified TOI loop. The remainder of the tick is searched for its earliest contact among all types, both fighters are rewound to that instant, the contact is resolved according to its interaction type, and the rest of the tick is carried forward from the post-resolution state before searching again. Body separation is part of this chronology, not a pre-contact pass, because a body-separation correction can move a fighter away from a sword tip and erase a stab that chronologically preceded the body overlap. A parry at `t = 0.20` has to decide whether the cut at `t = 0.34` happens, and sorting candidates found from the original poses would award a hit the parry prevented. The loop is bounded by `max_contacts_per_tick`; saturation holds the pair in contact and emits `CONTACT_SATURATED`. A contact is reported when it begins, never again on every substep it stays touching, so live play does not saturate.
- **A held contact stays solid (RIGID-CONTACT-001).** The first clash is an impulse; every tick after it the contact is a constraint (`RigidContactSolver`). Before the tick's motion is carried, a held blade or a sword resting on a living torso loses whatever closing speed the motors gave it; if it is driven past twice the 2 mm `contact_slop` mid-tick, or another contact's impulse changes its velocities, it is held again at that instant; a hold that _arrests_ a cut ends it in recovery — a cut that stops on a body or a guard stops there, and cutting again means winding back off it — but a hold the cut keeps beating aside does not: as long as the other blade gives way and the cut keeps travelling, it is still a cut, however slow (only once that blade reaches its stop and can give no further is the cut arrested). A contact that merely persists is never an interrupt, or the phase would quietly become a bind; and at the end of the tick any overlap beyond the slop is pushed out by moving θ and the bodies, never their speeds, so the correction adds no energy. The motor is what the fighter tries to do; the constraint is what the sword can do. "Driven deeper" means deeper than the contact was held: once re-held mid-tick, a contact is held again only after one more slop of depth, so a fast-turning contact's curvature drift is left to the overlap correction instead of re-held on every substep. A held sword near its guard stop has the stop inside the same solve, so the solve never counts on turning a sword the stop will catch.
- **A held contact has friction (BLADE-FRICTION-001).** Every sword contact, first clash and held alike, opposes sliding at the contact point with Coulomb friction: the accumulated tangent impulse never exceeds `μ` times the accumulated normal impulse, four fixed iterations, rows in fixed order. That one law is what gives slide, press and stick a physical basis. A fast glance whose sliding asks for more than `μ·Jn` keeps sliding; a slow one inside the cone stops; pressing harder grips harder, easing off grips less, and a contact that is separating has no normal impulse and so no friction at all. Stepping sideways along a held blade drags the other blade with it. Coefficients are material: steel on steel 0.20, a blade on cloth and flesh 0.05 (a cut resting on a torso still slides), — gameplay calibration seeds, not steel tables. Each held row starts the tick from the impulses it accumulated last tick, but only from the record that is the same contact point (RIGID-CONTACT-003, CONTACT-IDENTITY-001). When a fixed iteration count ends on a friction step, a last normal-only top-up restores every normal constraint and nothing follows it — a larger normal impulse only widens the friction cone. What friction changed, measured against a frictionless control on the same code (40 CPU duels): clashes +23 %, contacts held about a tick longer, a clash becomes a body hit within half a second less often (16.7 % → 14.4 %), damage flat, and sword-on-torso contacts still essentially all slides.
- **Why the constraint can be trusted.** Each solve uses one mass per coordinate (every body and sword at its contact-mechanics mass), so it is a projection in a single kinetic-energy metric: with the actuators still, a held contact can only take energy out, and equal and opposite impulses move no momentum. That is also why nothing clamps what it produces. Its physical normal impulse is recorded on the manifold (the quantity friction is bounded by); the overlap correction is pseudo-motion, recorded nowhere — a slightly deep overlap must never become fake friction pressure. Bodies answer contacts with their real mass, the same `1/m_body` in the effective mass and in `Δv = J/m_body`. Passive inertia on θ is the sword plus the hands at the grip. Plant and commitment do not scale it; a fighter who is hard to move while pressing is hard to move because the motors are still pushing, not because the sword got heavier.
- **What rigid contact changed in play.** Measured by running the same duels with the constraint inert: first impacts are bit-identical, but without it a large share of strong hits came from a blade that had just passed straight through the opponent's guard, or one embedded in the torso. Those hits no longer exist; guards hold, so fewer strong cuts land and rounds run longer.
- **One clash is one contact.** The `SwordContactSet` carries the blade lifecycle (`SEPARATED → CONTACTING → SEPARATING`) with `separation_epsilon` hysteresis, so blades leaning on each other do not re-strike every tick and a held pair generates no new first impacts. Separation is physical; the cooldown counter this replaced was not. Parts that genuinely come apart may clash again immediately — even inside the same tick.
- **The body has two sizes, for two jobs** (CONTACT-ROLES-001). `push_radius` (0.27 m) is the footprint that keeps bodies apart and margins the arena edge; `hurt_radius` (0.195 m, Wolf's gambeson measured at blade height) is the torso a blade strikes. Weapon→body contact and threat reach use the torso, so a blade can pass inside the footprint and miss, and a guard held in front of the torso is really in front of it. The push radius is never a hit test.
- **θ is the sword; the orbit is only where the hands are** (WEAPON-PATH-001 – 003, WEAPON-ORBIT-001 – 006). Riposte is still the one-degree-of-freedom 270° rotational sword it always was. The weapon angle θ ∈ [−135°, +135°] is the blade's horizontal angle from the combat heading, and the world yaw is always heading + θ. The old model swung the sword on a constant-radius circle round the navel, which buried two hands — and the pommel 19 cm behind them — in the fighter's own body. `WeaponAttackPath` only stretches that circle: a smooth, mirrored orbit that carries the hands farthest forward at the centre (0.63 m at Long Guard, a rounded top) and draws them toward the striking shoulder further round (0.30 m forward, 0.20 m out at the load), so the whole rigid sword clears the torso and the near arm never folds. Long Guard is the longest posture (LONG-GUARD-GEOMETRY-001): the hands farthest out and the blade straight down the heading, so both compound into the greatest point reach on the track. Its depth comes from Wolf's own rig — as far as both hands hold the grip with the off-hand arm still short of locking, shoulders unmoved — never from where an opponent would stand. The opponent is the physics' business: a centred point meets an advancing body before the bodies can meet, and walking cannot force a body through it; beat the point aside and the same walk closes (LONG-GUARD-INTERCEPT-001). That is point control from geometry alone, with no special brace, mass or retraction. It never turns the blade on its own, so it changes the arc's shape, not what an angle means: attack ranges, side history, charge travel, end-angle seating and POKE / THRUST / SLASH all read θ exactly as before, and Long Guard, Ready, Gather and Load are names for regions of θ, not physics states. A contact point's velocity is the ordinary rotation plus the hands' translation along the orbit. A malformed orbit — or one that puts any part of the sword inside the torso — invalidates the rules; nothing substitutes a different one.
- **The blades are steel, and each crossguard on its blade side** (STEEL-TOPOLOGY-001, GUARD-PROTECTION-001; rules 50 the blades alone, rules 52 the guard root's protective side). Rules 35–49 made the crossguard and the handle solid too (GUARD-CONTACT-001): up to eighteen points across nine feature pairs, concave hooks and slots, two held points on non-parallel parts locking the pivot, and a crossguard friction of 0.40 reaching both bodies — binds that ordinary input could not open. Replayed from the same recorded commands, blade-only steel took held contacts from 746 to 409, long holds from 192 to 111 and the longest from 1850 to 648 ticks; rules 52's protective guard roots, on those same commands, make 701 held contacts, 149 long and the longest 600 ticks (rules 51: 486, 133, 521). The longest is a blade-on-blade bind the recorded commands keep pressing: forked anywhere inside it, backing off or a lateral step parts it in 4–17 ticks. At rules 50–51 the crossguard passed through; rules 52 makes its blade side steel again as a bounded convex root against the other blade only — never guard on guard, so the hooks and locks of rules 35–49 cannot come back — and the handle and the hands are not combat geometry (TORSO-CIRCLE-001, rules 55; rules 50–54 wounded that grip without blocking). abb6a27 (rules 20) also had blade-only steel, with an authored consequence on top — a deflection interrupt, recovery up to 48 ticks, exposure damage, scripted binds; rules 50 restores the topology without any of it, and whether some of that consequence comes back is a question for after the blade-only human pass.
- **The blade wounds; the blade and the protected crossguard can defend** (DEFENSE-ROLE-001). The blade defends its wielder (`blade_protects`) and it wounds. The guard root defends on its protected side (`crossguard_protects`) and never wounds. Roles change what a contact means — whether it is a parry or a deflection — never when it happens. Hands and the handle have no role (TORSO-CIRCLE-001).
- **The torso circle is the only damage target** (TORSO-CIRCLE-001, rules 55; GRIP-NONBLOCKING-001 before that). `hurt_radius` is the circle the blade's rotational track is tested against, for both the contact and the damage entry. Hands, arms, and the handle are drawn and they position the sword; they do not wound, block, or create a contact. A crossguard still blocks on its blade side and never wounds. Rules 48 once tried a rigid 3.5 cm grip; it armoured the hands and was reverted. Rules 50–54 then wounded that grip without blocking. Rules 55 removes the wound.
- **At most two points a steel pair, every constraint kept** (TWO-POINT-001). Two convex pieces of steel in the plane touch at one point when they cross and at the two ends of their overlap when they lie along each other (always both ends — an interior point is implied by them), and both are solved from the first impact and in the hold by one exact block (`ContactLcp`). A real third constraint left out is still counted (`MeaningfulContactsDropped`), and the ship corpus pins it at zero. `measure` is exact near contact and conservative beyond `EXACT_WITHIN`, never a global distance. Three pairs at two points each make six: where a guard point and a blade point are one constraint, they are one row — the blade's unless the guard's surface is in front. (Rules 37–49 kept two points on each of nine feature pairs across blade, crossguard and handle; that went with the steel.)
- **Where the blades touch is recorded, not reconstructed** (CONTACT-MANIFOLD-001). The contact set is the one owner of sword contact: fixed-capacity points, at most two per steel pair, each with its pair and its blade fractions (0 at the blade base, 1 at the point; a guard root's own side reads 0), its own normal, and its solved impulses. Strong/middle/weak is a reading of the fraction, never the contact's identity, so a contact sliding down a blade stays one contact.
- **A contact point is the same contact by geometry, not by slot** (CONTACT-IDENTITY-001). When one end of a line contact leaves, the survivor changes slot — so one small fixed matcher decides identity: the least movement along both blades, one to one. Its warm-start impulse, its age, whether it persisted and its stable `contact_id` (what sparks and scrape loops key on) all follow that one answer.
- **One energy law.** The energy a sword contact takes out is the drop in kinetic energy of everything the solve moved, in the masses it moved them with — however many points, restitution and friction included. For a single frictionless contact that is exactly `½(1 − e²)·v_n²/k`. Separated geometry simply leaves no points. Every cap in the pipeline is fixed; reaching one is counted on its own counter (`ContactDiagnostics`, each surfaced as its own `Riposte/*` custom monitor — never one summed number) and takes a deterministic fallback instead of buying more work. A fallback is legal, so adversarial play may reach one; representative play should not, and the ship corpus pins each at zero — except the re-hold budget, which a fast slide along a turning blade can honestly spend: the ship corpus accepts that one class, classified and within its measured baseline (CONTACT-REHOLD-FALLBACK-001). A budget is never raised just to make a counter read zero.
- **Weapon→body uses an entry-contact lifecycle.** `WeaponBodyContact` per attacker tracks `OUTSIDE → ENTERED → INSIDE → EXITED → OUTSIDE` with separation hysteresis. Damage is applied only on entry (`OUTSIDE → ENTERED`). While the weapon remains inside the body volume, no further damage is dealt. The lifecycle rearms after a genuine geometric exit. This prevents a penetrating sword from dealing repeated damage, and does not use an invulnerability timer — geometry decides when the weapon has left the body.
- **Blade on blade**: a sequential-impulse solve in weapon coordinates (`SwordContactSolver`) from relative contact-point velocity, each weapon's lever (its Jacobian on the normal), both effective inertias, and the normal — a normal stage that never pulls, then a Coulomb-clamped tangent stage, for a constant number of iterations; with `μ = 0` it is exactly the one-shot impulse `(1 + e)·closing / K` at any iteration count. Faster blades, heavier blades, and blades met on a shorter lever displace the other. One arrest law governs both a first clash and a held contact (RIGID-CONTACT-002): a swing ends into recovery only when the contact opposed it (`Δθ̇·dir < 0`) and left it stopped, reversed, or pinned — under the wielder's `control_speed` with nothing gained over the tick despite its motor — no ratio, no `deflect_fraction`, no timer. A cut pinned against a torso or a stopped guard that only creeps as it yields is therefore over, never pressed for seconds; a cut launched out of a bind is gaining on its first tick and stays a cut. There is no bind state and no bind winner: two blades that stay together are simply a persisted rigid contact, re-solved every tick and escapable the moment either fighter moves. Whether that persistent contact reads as a glance, a slide or a bind is derived vocabulary — Coulomb friction is what makes sliding and sticking physically different — never a solver mode.
- **Parry**: classified, not pressed, and physical (PARRY-001): the defender's sword met the cut first, with a real impulse, and arrested it. The tempo it leaves — the riposte advantage, the attacker's time-to-threat minus the defender's — rides on the event as a separate reading and may be zero or negative: a parry need not win the exchange.
- **Body→body**: inverse-mass impulse with low restitution plus bounded Coulomb tangential friction. A heavier fighter absorbs less velocity. The tangential impulse is bounded by `body_friction × normal_impulse` (Coulomb's law), so a glancing shoulder bump sheds some lateral speed but cannot halt a side-step. Body collision MUST NOT directly modify weapon angular state on either fighter.
- **Sword→Body (PHYS-008)**: coupled through the contact Jacobian. Target movement participates in relative contact kinematics (closing velocity, severity), and the body always receives damage, displacement, stagger, and stamina shock. The weapon receives an opposite reactive angular impulse from the same solved impulse that pushes the target (`Δθ̇ = J·c/M_θ`, IMPACT-PHYSICS-001), whatever the contact is classified as. A transverse slash has a long lever and is checked; a point driven along its blade has almost none and drives through; a point met off its axis is turned aside. Bracing and commitment change how hard the motors push, not how heavy the sword is. A lethal blow's clean entry is the lifecycle's (`penetration_resistance_fraction`, then `PENETRATING`), not a suppressed reaction.
- **Defense is chronological (COMBAT-013)**: determined entirely by the TOI contact loop, not by persisted flags. When a blade↔blade contact resolves before a weapon→body contact, the blade impulse changes the weapon trajectory and the body contact is re-evaluated from the post-deflection state. If the thrust classification no longer qualifies, the defense succeeded through physics. If the thrust still qualifies after the deflection, the point drove through and the thrust lands. There is no `attack_episode_id` or `intercepted_episode_id` in authoritative state.

## Debug

F3 (debug builds) shows distance, closing speed, orbit, each fighter's phase/charge/commitment/stability/time-to-threat, and the last contact (`game/src/presentation/hud/debug_overlay.gd`). Because every consequence decomposes into named physical quantities, the overlay can explain any hit: body speed and acceleration, blade-point speed, target speed, closing speed, structural coupling, effective mass, impulse, severity, edge alignment, exposure, and final quality. A debug vector mode draws fighter velocity, blade contact velocity, target velocity, and relative contact velocity as distinguishable arrows (never colour alone).

## Contact classification (COMBAT-010, COMBAT-011)

Every body contact is classified exactly once: `SLASH`, `POKE`, `THRUST`, or `GRAZE` (`StrikeResult.ContactKind` in `game/src/domain/combat/strike_result.gd`). The event carries `StrikeResult.KIND_*` under `DuelEventKeys.CONTACT_KIND`. Classification happens **after** the contact is geometrically resolved — there is no stab button, no thrust animation, no `STAB_ATTACK` phase, and no separate stab damage multiplier. A point strike exists only when the physical state produces one.

**POINT_STRIKE** is the parent classification for point-first contacts. Two sub-kinds describe how the contact arose:

- **POKE**: point-first body contact during normal movement (walking/standing). The fighter's forward velocity is at or below normal speed. Common, and usually survivable — but a POKE MAY kill if physical severity reaches the lethal threshold (e.g. opponent dashing onto the point).
- **THRUST**: point-first body contact with forward burst and sword-to-motion alignment. Rare. A THRUST is an **instant kill** regardless of HP or severity. This is an explicit game rule: the forward burst velocity and sword alignment are the criteria. Defense is handled by the chronological TOI solver — a blade contact that precedes the body contact physically redirects the weapon, and the thrust is re-evaluated from the post-deflection trajectory.

### Point threat and emergent point strikes

The sword tip is a physical point at `T = P + L × [cos φ, sin φ]`. Its velocity has three independent components:

```text
v_tip = v_fighter + ω_body × r_pivot + ω_weapon × r_tip
```

So a completely motionless sword relative to the fighter can still have substantial point velocity from a forward dash, and a stationary fighter creates tip velocity through weapon rotation alone. Both components may act simultaneously, and target motion participates through the relative quantity `v_rel = v_tip − v_target`.

This means point strikes emerge naturally from:

- walking or dashing forward with a point-forward sword;
- standing still while the opponent runs onto the point;
- both fighters closing simultaneously;
- a post-deflection sword angle that aligns the point forward;
- a combined rotational-translational entry at the end of a swing.

### Axial relative velocity and alignment

The sword's axial direction from hilt toward point is `â = (T − P) / |T − P|`. Axial closing velocity `v_axial = v_rel · â` measures how fast the point is approaching the target along the blade's own axis. **Thrust alignment** `A = max(0, v_axial) / (|v_rel| + ε)` is the fraction of total relative motion that is axial: `A ≈ 1` is a clean thrust, `A ≈ 0` is a crosswise slash. **Incidence quality** `G = max(0, −â · n̂)` measures whether the point is entering the body surface rather than grazing past it.

### Point-strike qualification

A body contact is classified as a point strike (POKE or THRUST) only when **all** of:

1. Point-region contact first (`blade_fraction ≥ tip_region_start`)
2. Positive axial relative closing velocity
3. Sufficient thrust alignment
4. Sufficient incidence quality

Classification is purely geometric — severity affects damage, not classification. A weak aligned tip contact is a weak POKE, not a GRAZE. GRAZE means tangential geometry (low contact quality), not low energy.

The THRUST sub-kind additionally requires the attacker to be in an active forward burst with burst direction aligned to the sword axis. Without the burst, the same geometry classifies as POKE.

The chronological TOI loop is critical: a parry at `t = 0.17` changes the trajectory, so a point-strike candidate at `t = 0.28` must disappear or recompute.

### One physical severity curve, divergent lethality

All point strikes — POKE and THRUST — share **one physical severity model** for the base evaluation. Severity is computed from axial relative velocity, effective mass, incidence quality, and structural coupling through the existing `ImpactModel`. There is no `STAB_MULTIPLIER`. Lethality diverges:

- **POKE**: severity below the lethal threshold produces ordinary point damage from the compressive damage curve. Severity at or above the lethal threshold produces an instant kill. A poke at walking speeds is usually survivable; an opponent dashing onto the point can be lethal.
- **THRUST**: instant kill — no damage roll, no HP check, no severity threshold. The forward burst velocity and sword alignment are the criteria. The chronological TOI solver handles defense: if a blade contact at `t = 0.17` redirected the weapon, a re-evaluated thrust at `t = 0.23` may no longer qualify as a thrust at all. If it still qualifies, the physics says the point drove through. An exact TOI tie between blade defense and body contact resolves as defense-wins.

**Countering a thrust is physical.** If an opponent leaves their point forward and closes, the player swings, the blade reaches the opponent's blade, the impulse resolves, the stabbing sword gets knocked off line, and the point/body candidate disappears. There is no parry button — the counter is physical.

TOI attribution is unchanged: a lethal point strike at `t = 0.32` loses to any lethal contact at `t = 0.27`, and an exact tie is a draw.

### Shared contact kinematics (PHYS-007)

All contact types — blade/blade, blade/body slash, tip/body poke, tip/body thrust — derive contact-point velocity from the same kinematic model. There is no separate stab velocity formula. Forward movement coherence (`v_fighter · â_sword`) participates through structural coupling, not as a separate multiplier, because the translational contribution is already present in `v_tip`. Double-counting is the central danger: forward speed must appear exactly once, in the tip velocity computation, never again as a damage bonus.

### Point legibility

There is no "STAB READY" indicator. Players learn to see sword orientation, point direction, body movement, and open lines. The blade is the interface. Presentation derives trail shape from kinematics: high thrust alignment naturally produces a narrow directional streak rather than a broad curved ribbon, with no combat state change. A qualifying point-strike contact produces distinct concentrated feedback — short strong hitstop, directional impact cue, minimal knockback — because impulse and lethality are different quantities.

### CPU and point strikes

CPU does not receive a `stab()` action. It uses the same movement and attack primitives a human does. Hard CPU may observe that its point is aligned and the opponent's line is open, and select forward movement that the physics then converts into a point strike. Easy CPU misses these opportunities. This is emergent tactical depth, not an authored technique.

## Condition and stamina (CONDITION-001, STAMINA-004)

Two things degrade a fighter: damage, for the round, and fatigue, moment to moment. (Rules 47–52 had no fatigue resource at all, STAMINA-003; the old stamina pool scaled nothing and was removed. Rules 53 brings stamina back as a real mechanic.)

### Stamina (STAMINA-004)

Stamina `s ∈ [0, 1]` is full at every round start. The motors' own work drains it — each motor's driving work (and a third of its braking work) against a reference energy derived from the fighter: one full acceleration to top speed, one spin-up of the heading, one full-charge swing — and a dash's start costs a fixed fifth of the bar, once. Easing off (a tick whose exertion stays under a small ceiling) recovers it, a full bar in about eight seconds. Contact impulses are never exertion.

Two gates read the bar at the start of the tick: a dash starts only if it can be paid for (a refused dash is dropped, never buffered, and costs nothing), and a held charge earns wind-back credit only while the bar is above zero — it stops accumulating, it is never released for the player, and the release fires with the charge already earned. Fatigue weakens voluntary force: `force_scale` is the damage term times `0.75 + 0.25s`, never below `0.70 × 0.75` together. That includes the weapon's swing, wind-up, brake, stagger hold, and the soft zone's share of wind-up, so a tired blade accelerates less and a braking tick takes off less speed. A constant-deceleration reading of that one tick is a longer stop; the soft zone, the guard limit, and a phase change can end the coast before any such distance is travelled. It does not include the guard return or the neutral hold, and it does not move a speed ceiling, the tracking cap, or when a command takes effect. A tired cut does less only because the arm drove the blade less hard; stamina never multiplies damage. A tick runs on its starting stamina and settles once at its end, so a dash's cost never weakens its own start.

In Hard v Hard play the bar averages about 0.55, a round's low has a median near 0.13, and a fighter is under one dash's cost about a fifth of the time: dashing and swinging both spend it, and a fighter who lets up gets it back.

### Condition (CONDITION-001)

Damage does not change the laws of physics; it changes how much voluntary force, speed and tracking a fighter can produce within them. Once per tick, before any motor runs, `ConditionModel` freezes each fighter's `ConditionState` from health alone (`h = health / max_health`):

| Scale            | Law            | At half health | Multiplies                                                                                       |
| ---------------- | -------------- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `force_scale`    | `0.70 + 0.30h` | 0.85           | every voluntary force and torque (footwork, braking, burst, turn, swing, brake, wind-back, hold) |
| `speed_scale`    | `0.85 + 0.15h` | 0.925          | every voluntary target speed (top speed, bursts, swing and wind-back targets)                    |
| `tracking_scale` | `0.80 + 0.20h` | 0.90           | the heading-rate cap `turn_speed_max`                                                            |

Linear, exactly 1 at full health, floors only at zero. Momentum is never rescaled — on the injury tick every velocity is bit-identical — and mass, inertia, geometry, reach, friction, perception and reaction never change, so a wounded fighter is visibly weaker while every command still lands on its tick. `CommitmentModel` is unchanged: injury is never counted twice. The CPU, the edge-safety predictor and the threat estimate read the same model (stamina included); the player HUD shows the condition band and the stamina bar, and the F3 overlay shows the three scales.

### Condition classification

Health is projected into four coarse bands (`HEALTHY`, `HURT`, `WOUNDED`, `CRITICAL`) for presentation and CPU. The simulation never reads condition; it is a downstream projection of continuous health. The HUD displays condition labels instead of exact HP.

### Presentation pipeline

`SnapshotProjector` copies the three condition scales (computed by the domain's `ConditionModel`, never rederived) and the condition band into `PresentationFighter` once per tick. Presentation reads these read-only facts for condition indicators and the debug overlay — it never writes them back. Stamina and the dash cost are projected beside them (STAMINA-004). Point-strike contacts (`BODY_POKE`, `BODY_THRUST`) produce distinct audio cues alongside the `BODY_HIT` feedback so the player hears the contact kind; hitstop and camera impulse are already sized by the physics on the hit itself.
